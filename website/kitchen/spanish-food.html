Busy Kitchen · the six kitchens

Spanish and Latin American food: the dishes people cook at home

Two continents at one table.

🍳 Open Busy Kitchen free

The Spanish-language kitchen spans two continents. In Spain, Romans left olive oil, wheat and wine; the centuries of Al-Andalus brought rice, saffron, citrus, almonds and the art of sweets that convents kept alive. The yearly matanza fed Castile through the winter. The oldest cookbooks are in Catalan. After 1492, tomatoes, potatoes and peppers crossed the ocean and changed everything, down to the paprika in the chorizo.

In Latin America the base is older still: maize, beans, squash, chilli, potato, cassava and cacao, grown and cooked for thousands of years. Mexico's traditional cuisine became world heritage in 2010; Peru's ceviche followed in 2023, and the Caribbean's cassava bread, casabe, in 2024. On that base came Spanish, African, Italian, Chinese and Japanese cooks, which is why Lima has lomo saltado and Buenos Aires has gnocchi on the 29th.

Meals run late and long. In Spain lunch is the main meal and dinner comes after nine; the talk after the meal has its own name, sobremesa. Paella is eaten from the pan. In Mexico families build an ofrenda for their dead; in Venezuela the whole family makes hallacas together at Christmas.

How to say it

sobremesa (so-breh-MEH-sah), calçots (kal-SOTS), hallaca (ah-YAH-kah).

Its regions

  • Andalucía Cold bread soups (gazpacho, salmorejo, ajoblanco, porra, pipirrana), fried fish, almadraba tuna, Iberian ham from the Huelva and Córdoba sierras, and convent sweets with almond and honey.
  • Aragón Lamb (Ternasco IGP), chilindrón, borage and cardoon, Teruel ham, guirlache and frutas de Aragón.
  • Asturias Bean stews (fabada, pote), cider culture, close to 50 cheeses (Cabrales), rice pudding, frixuelos and casadielles.
  • Illes Balears Pork and lard (sobrassada, ensaïmada), frit and tumbet, lobster caldereta in Menorca, flaó and bullit de peix in Ibiza, dried fish in Formentera.
  • Canarias Wrinkled potatoes with mojo, gofio, sancocho, puchero, rabbit in salmorejo, potaje de berros, bienmesabe.
  • Cantabria Dairy (sobaos, quesada pasiega, cheeses), cocido montañés, Santoña anchovies, sorropotún.
  • Castilla-La Mancha Pisto, migas, galianos (gazpacho manchego), morteruelo, partridge, garlic soup, Manchego cheese, Toledo marzipan.
  • Castilla y León The "España del asado" (suckling pig, lechazo), botillo, hornazo, cocido maragato, garlic soup, cured meats, convent yemas.
  • Catalunya Pa amb tomàquet, escudella, canelons, calçots with romesco, escalivada, suquet, crema catalana, panellets, monas.
  • Comunitat Valenciana Rice in every form (paella, arròs a banda, al forn, amb costra), ollas, horchata with fartons, turrón from Xixona and Alicante.
  • Extremadura Iberian pig from the dehesa, pimentón de la Vera, Torta del Casar, migas, lamb caldereta, convent sweets.
  • Galicia Octopus á feira, empanada, lacón con grelos, caldo, shellfish from the rías, tarta de Santiago, filloas.
  • Madrid Cocido, callos, the squid sandwich, San Isidro rosquillas, churros with chocolate.
  • Región de Murcia Huerta vegetables (zarangollo, michirones), caldero, pastel de carne, marinera, paparajotes.
  • Navarra Vegetables from La Ribera (piquillo, asparagus, artichoke, menestra), pochas, chilindrón, ajoarriero, Roncal cheese.
  • País Vasco / Euskadi Cod (pil-pil, vizcaína), marmitako, kokotxas, txangurro, txuleta, pintxos, goxua.

How people eat

  • Meal times Breakfast is light: coffee, milk or chocolate with biscuits, magdalenas, toast or churros. A mid-morning snack follows, which some regions call almuerzo. The main meal (comida) runs from about 14:00 to 15:30. Merienda is around 18:00, and dinner (cena) between 20:30 and 23:00.
  • Why so late? Franco moved Spain to Central European Time on 17 March 1940, to line up with German-occupied Europe, and made it permanent in 1942. Some argue this explains the late meals; others, such as the physicist Jorge Mira, say work schedules would just shift with the clock. The link is debated, not proven.
  • Sobremesa Is the conversation that lingers at the table after a meal.
  • Menú del día It was created in 1964 as the menú turístico by Manuel Fraga's Ministry of Information and Tourism: several courses, bread and a drink at a capped price. Price caps by category came in May 1965. It was renamed menú del día after Fraga left the ministry in 1969, and stopped being compulsory in 2010. Its average price was €14.2 at the end of 2025. It is usually served from about 13:30 to 16:30.
  • Tapeo Tapas are small plates eaten before, during or between meals, often free with a drink. Examples are boquerones en vinagre, mussels in escabeche, gildas, albóndigas, callos and torreznos. In the Basque Country and Pamplona the bar snack is the pintxo. Andalusian bars keep a seasonal tapa list.
  • Eating from the pan Paella is traditionally eaten straight from the pan. Restaurants traditionally offer it on Thursdays.
  • Wooden plates Pulpo á feira is served on wooden plates, which keep it warm.
  • Cider pouring Asturian cider is poured from a height (escanciar) in sidrerías and llagares. The drink itself is out of scope, but it shapes the meal.
  • Order of courses Cocido maragato is served "in a different order than usual": meats first, broth last. A cocido is usually eaten in "vuelcos" (soup first, then chickpeas and vegetables, then meats).
  • Plate-cut suckling pig In Segovia, cochinillo is carved with the edge of a plate to show how tender it is.
  • Guachinches These informal Canary eating houses serve home dishes.
  • Merienda sweets Christmas sweets (turrón, polvorón, mazapán, mantecado) are eaten at merienda and after meals.
  • Comida corrida (Mexico) A fixed, cheap, several-course midday meal at a fonda, usually between 12:00 and 15:00: soup, rice or pasta, a main dish, agua fresca and a small dessert. "Corrida" refers to the run of courses, not to eating fast. Its origin is dated variously to the Porfiriato or the early 20th century.
  • The ofrenda (Día de Muertos) Families set out the dead person's favourite dishes and drinks, together with pan de muerto and calabaza en tacha, from about 31 October to 2 November. The ofrenda is traditionally taken down after 3 November. In Yucatán the same days are called Hanal Pixán ("food of the souls"), with the pib earth-oven dish.
  • Rosca, figurine and tamales On 6 January (Día de Reyes) the family cuts the Rosca de Reyes. Whoever finds the Niño Dios figurine becomes its godparent and hosts tamales and atole on 2 February, Día de la Candelaria.
  • Making Christmas food together Venezuelan families make hallacas together, with a production line for dough, filling, plantain leaves and tying. Puerto Rican and Dominican pasteles. Guatemalan and Costa Rican tamales are made the same way.
  • Novena de aguinaldos (Colombia, 16–24 December) Neighbours and families pray the nine-night novena and share natilla, buñuelos and arroz con leche.
  • Nochebuena (Cuba and the diaspora, 24 December) Lechón asado, often roasted in a caja china, is served with congrí, yuca con mojo, buñuelos and turrón. Sources say high prices and shortages now put this meal out of reach for many families in Cuba.
  • La once (Chile) An early-evening "tea" of bread (marraqueta or hallulla) with spreads, plus tea or coffee. In a Cadem survey cited by UOH, 76% of Chileans said they take it daily. The origin of the name is disputed. The "aguardiente has eleven letters" story is popular but unproven; one researcher links the name to a light snack eaten around 11 a.m..
  • Mate and tereré rounds (Río de la Plata, Paraguay) Argentina's Ley 26.871 (2013) defines mate as yerba mate steeped in hot water and drunk through a bombilla, and names it the national infusion. 30 November is Día Nacional del Mate (Ley 27.117). In Paraguay, cold tereré with pohã ñana herbs is a shared daily ritual, recognised by UNESCO.
  • Asado (Argentina, Uruguay) Grilling over embers is the main celebration meal of the Southern Cone.
  • Sobremesa Staying at the table to talk after the meal is a widely described Hispanic custom.
  • Ñoquis del 29 (Argentina, Uruguay, Paraguay) Gnocchi are eaten on the 29th of each month, often with a banknote under the plate. The custom came with Italian immigration.
  • Earth-oven feasts Communal cooking in pits is held for gatherings and special days: Pachamanca in Peru, with a national day in early February. Curanto on Chiloé. Pib in Yucatán.
  • Picanterías (Arequipa) Traditional lunch houses serving rocoto relleno, chupe de camarones and other spicy dishes.
  • The jícara and the clay pot Pinol, pinolillo, tejate and café de olla are drunk from gourds or clay vessels. Café de olla is named after the clay pot.

In the cupboard

  • Olive oil The base fat of the south and east. Andalucía uses the most of any region. DOP oils include Garrigues and Siurana in Catalonia, Bajo Aragón and Mallorca.
  • Pork and lard Matanza products such as chorizo, morcilla, lacón, sobrassada, botillo, cecina and Iberian ham. Lard (saïm) gives the ensaïmada its name.
  • Pimentón (paprika) Vera (Extremadura) and Murcia. Extremadura's tourism text says the region's conquistadors brought paprika to Spain.
  • Saffron From the Al-Andalus period; La Mancha. Azafrán de La Mancha DOP.
  • Rice Valencia, the Delta de l'Ebre, and Calasparra in Murcia (DOP, including the bomba variety).
  • Legumes Fabes, chickpeas, lentils (Armuña), pochas, caparrones, alubias.
  • Salt cod (bacalao) The Lenten and inland fish, kept well and eaten everywhere.
  • Bread Stale bread is the base of gazpacho, salmorejo, migas, sopa de ajo and torrijas.
  • Almonds and honey Turrón, marzipan, polvorones, panellets, ajoblanco.
  • Garden vegetables Tomato, peppers (piquillo, Padrón/Herbón, choricero, ñora), artichoke, asparagus, cardoon, borage, calçot.
  • Seafood Octopus, mussels, barnacles, anchovy, bonito, hake, tuna.
  • Island staples Gofio (toasted cereal flour), small potatoes, mojo; chufa (tiger nut) for horchata.
  • Cheeses Manchego, Cabrales, Idiazabal, Roncal, Torta del Casar, Mahón-Menorca, Tetilla.
  • Maize in all its forms Mexico: nixtamalised maize for masa, cacahuazintle for pozole, and huitlacoche. Peru: purple maize for chicha morada and mazamorra. The DO Maíz Blanco Gigante del Cusco (2005). Andes and Southern Cone: choclo, the large-kernel fresh maize. Mexico: DO Cacao Grijalva, Café Chiapas and Café Veracruz, Chile Yahualica, Mango Ataúlfo del Soconusco, Vainilla (Papantla).
  • Beans and pulses Black and red beans across the region. The DO Pallar de Ica in Peru (2007). Pigeon peas (gandules), which reached the Caribbean from India via Africa.
  • Chillies (ají, chile) Mexican chiles (ancho, mulato, pasilla, chipotle, poblano, habanero). The DO Chile habanero de la Península de Yucatán. Peruvian ají amarillo, ají panca and rocoto. Chilean merkén.
  • Tubers and roots Potato: hundreds of native varieties in the Andes; the ajiaco uses several of them. Cassava (yuca): for casabe, chipa and carimañolas. Malanga, yautía, ñame and otoe in the Caribbean and Panama. Oca, olluco and chuño (freeze-dried potato) in the Andes.
  • Plantain Green and ripe: mofongo, mangú, bolón de verde, patacones, tostones.
  • Squash Including the DO Loche de Lambayeque in Peru (2010).
  • Cacao and chocolate Mole, champurrado, Oaxacan chocolate. The DO Cacao Amazonas Perú (2016). The DO Cacao Grijalva in Mexico. Cacao porcelana of the Sierra Nevada in Colombia is a Slow Food Presidium.
  • Andean grains Quinoa, amaranth (alegría), maca (DO Maca Junín-Pasco).
  • Dairy and cheeses Queso fresco and queso costeño. The DO Queso Paipa in Colombia. Paraguayan queso Paraguay in sopa paraguaya. Dulce de leche in Argentina and Uruguay.
  • Sweeteners Piloncillo, panela and papelón.
  • Leaves for wrapping and cooking Corn husk, banana or plantain leaf (hallaca, pasteles, tamales), bijao (juane), maguey and nalca (curanto).
  • Seasonings and condiments Achiote (annatto) and naranja agria in Yucatán. Culantro, ajíes dulces and sofrito in the Caribbean. Mojo criollo: sour orange, garlic and pork fat. Oregano: DO Orégano de Tacna (2024). Olives: DO Aceituna de Tacna.
  • Seafood and fish Limes for ceviche. The DO Limón de Pica, Chile's first Sello de Origen (2012). Bonito and corvina. Juan Fernández lobster and golden crab (Sellos de Origen). Dried salt cod for Lent and Christmas.
  • Meat Pork is everywhere at feasts (lechón, pernil, lechona). Beef in the Southern Cone. Cuy (guinea pig) in the Andes. Kid goat in northern Mexico.
  • Infusions and coffee Yerba mate (Ilex paraguariensis). Coffee DOs: Café de Colombia plus regional DOs (Nariño, Cauca, Huila, Tolima, Santander, Sierra Nevada) Café Villa Rica and Café Machu Picchu–Huadquiña in Peru Café Veracruz and Café Chiapas in Mexico.
  • Colombian sweets with DO Bocadillo veleño (guava paste), Achiras del Huila, Cholupa del Huila.
