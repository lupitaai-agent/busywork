# The marketing foundation

*The base under every marketing activity in Busywork and Olaf. Written in our own words from
proven marketing science: decades of research into how people really buy and what makes brands
grow, across hundreds of categories and many countries. We apply the findings and explain them in
plain words when a decision depends on them.*

---

## 1. Why a foundation at all

Most marketing advice is opinion. This foundation is built on patterns that show up again and
again in buying data, across categories, countries and decades: groceries and banking, cars and
software, luxury and B2B. They behave like laws: you can't wish them away, only work with them.

A small business doesn't have the budget to learn by expensive mistakes. Knowing how buyers
actually behave tells you where marketing money works, and where it disappears without a trace.

---

## 2. How buyers actually behave

### 2.1 Bigger brands have more buyers, who are a bit more loyal (Double Jeopardy)
Brands with a smaller market share suffer twice: they have far fewer buyers, *and* those buyers buy
them slightly less often. The difference between big and small brands is mostly in the *number* of
buyers, not in how loyal they are.

**For a small business:** you are not small because your customers don't love you enough. You are
small because not enough people buy from you yet. Growth comes from more buyers.

### 2.2 Most of your customers are light customers
In every category, buying frequency follows the same shape: a few people buy you often, and a long
tail buys you rarely, maybe once a year or once ever. Over a longer period, the light buyers add up
to a large share of sales. The famous "80% of sales from 20% of customers" is in reality closer to
60/20, and the heavy 20% changes from year to year as people's lives change.

**For a small business:** a marketing plan that only talks to existing, frequent customers ignores
where much of next year's revenue comes from. The customer who came once last year is a growth
opportunity, not a lost cause.

### 2.3 Loyalty exists, but it is shared and shallow
Buyers are loyal to a *repertoire* of brands, not to one. Your customers also buy from your
competitors, and theirs buy from you. This is normal, not a failure.

### 2.4 You share customers with competitors in line with their size (Duplication of Purchase)
A brand shares the most customers with the biggest competitors, simply because they have the most
customers. There are rarely hidden "segments" where your brand competes only with one rival.

**For a small business:** you don't need a niche persona to explain who buys from you. Your
customers look like your competitors' customers: ordinary buyers of the category.

### 2.5 Attitudes follow behaviour
People who buy a brand say nicer things about it, because they know it. Liking is mostly a result of
buying, not the cause. Satisfaction surveys of your own customers tell you about your customers, not
about why non-customers don't choose you.

### 2.6 Word of mouth follows size
Bigger brands get talked about more, positively and negatively, because more people use them. Both
positive and negative word of mouth matter roughly equally per mention. Current users create most of
the positive talk; non-users more often the negative.

### 2.7 Most buyers are not in the market right now (the 95:5 idea)
For most categories, only a small share of potential buyers is ready to buy at any moment. In
business-to-business markets, published B2B research estimated it can be as little
as about 5% in a given quarter (the exact share depends on how often people buy). The other 95% will
buy later. Marketing that only chases today's buyers misses the people who will buy next month,
next season or next year.

**For a small business:** the job of most marketing is to be remembered when the moment comes. If
people only hear of you while they are already buying, you are too late for most of them.

## 3. What makes a brand grow

### 3.1 Growth comes mainly from more buyers (penetration)
Brands that grow do so mostly by gaining buyers. Loyalty and frequency rise a little as a brand grows
(Double Jeopardy), but they follow penetration; they don't drive it.

**What this means:** the main marketing question is always *how do more people who buy this
category come to know us, think of us, and find it easy to buy from us?*

### 3.2 Two kinds of availability
Buyers choose brands that are easy to think of and easy to buy.

- **Mental availability:** the chance that your brand comes to mind in buying situations.
- **Physical availability:** how easy you are to find, notice and buy, when the moment comes.

Every marketing activity should build one or both. If it builds neither, question it.

### 3.3 The working rules
These rules translate the research into behaviour (our phrasing):

1. **Reach all the buyers of your category, continuously.** Not a narrow persona; everyone who might
   buy what you sell in the area you serve. Keep a presence all year rather than in rare bursts.
2. **Make it easy to buy.** Build mental and physical availability, and remove the reasons people
   don't buy (price confusion, hard booking, closed at the wrong times, no reviews, slow website).
3. **Get noticed.** Ordinary-looking marketing is ignored. Attention is the entry ticket.
4. **Build and refresh memories.** Make it unmistakable *who* the message is from, and link the brand
   to the moments people need the category.
5. **Use distinctive brand assets.** Colours, logo, characters, sounds and phrases that people know
   are yours, used every time.
6. **Be consistent, but not boring.** Keep the brand cues the same; vary the stories.
7. **Stay competitive.** Price, quality, convenience and service don't need to be unique, but they
   must not give buyers a reason to walk away.

## 4. Mental availability and category entry points

### 4.1 Memory, not persuasion
Advertising mostly works by refreshing and building memories, not by convincing people with
arguments. A buyer meets thousands of brands. The ones that come to mind fastest, in the right
situation, get chosen.

### 4.2 Category entry points (CEPs)
A CEP is a situation, need or cue that makes someone think of the category: the moment they start
looking. The more CEPs your brand is linked to, and the stronger the links, the more often it
comes to mind.

Find CEPs by asking about the buying situation:
- **Why:** what need or problem? (*"my laptop died before a deadline"*)
- **When:** time of year, day, life event? (*"before the holidays"*, *"we just moved"*)
- **Where:** location or setting? (*"near the station"*, *"working from home"*)
- **With or for whom:** alone, for family, for a client, a gift?
- **With what:** what else is going on or being bought? (*"with a new website"*)
- **How they feel:** stressed, celebrating, curious, bored?

Use the buyer's own words, not internal jargon. Prioritise CEPs that are common among category
buyers and where competitors are not yet strongly linked.

### 4.3 Measuring mental availability (simple versions of the standard research metrics)
Ask a sample of category buyers which brands they associate with each priority CEP (a list of
brands, tick any that fit). Then:

- **Mental penetration:** the % of category buyers who link your brand to at least one CEP.
- **Network size:** among those, how many CEPs your brand is linked to on average.
- **Mental market share:** your share of all brand–CEP links given in the survey, across all
  brands. Over time this tends to track your share of sales.

For a small business, a yearly survey of 30–100 local category buyers (customers *and*
non-customers) already shows direction.

## 5. Distinctive brand assets

Distinctive assets are the non-name cues that trigger your brand: colours, logo, shapes, a
character, a tagline, a sound, a typeface, a style of photography.

An asset works when it is:
- **Famous:** many category buyers link it to your brand.
- **Unique:** it doesn't also make them think of a competitor.

Plot each element on the fame × uniqueness grid:

| | Low fame | High fame |
|---|---|---|
| **High uniqueness** | **Invest**: promising, use it more to build fame | **Use or lose**: a real asset, use it everywhere |
| **Low uniqueness** | **Test or ignore**: not working yet | **Avoid**: famous, but shared with others, it may help them |

Rules that follow:
- Choose a few assets and use them *every* time, in the same way.
- Never refresh or redesign a working asset for the sake of novelty. Many brands lost recognition
  (and sales) that way.
- Generic category cues (a tooth for dentists, a house for estate agents) are not distinctive.
- Test assets by showing them without the name and asking which brand they belong to.

## 6. Physical availability

Being easy to buy has three parts:
- **Presence:** you're there when and where people buy. Opening hours, delivery area, online
  booking, marketplaces, stockists, a working website, a Google Business Profile.
- **Relevance:** what you offer fits what buyers need there and then: right products, sizes,
  prices, payment options, response times.
- **Prominence:** you're easy to notice and find: shelf position, map ranking, reviews, signage,
  search results, a website that loads fast and shows what to do next.

**For a small business:** often the cheapest growth is fixing physical availability: a complete
and active Google Business Profile, accurate opening hours, being bookable online, answering fast,
clear prices, easy payment, being listed where your category is searched.

## 7. Advertising and communication

### 7.1 Reach beats frequency
Reaching *more* category buyers is worth more than hitting the same people more often. Light buyers
and non-buyers are only reached by broad reach.

### 7.2 Continuous presence beats bursts
Because buyers enter the market at different times, being present all year (even at a low level)
catches more of them than one big campaign followed by silence.

### 7.3 Targeting, used correctly
Targeting is useful for *where* category buyers are (geography, channels, the category itself) and
for avoiding obvious waste. It is harmful when it shrinks the audience to a small persona of
"ideal" customers and ignores everyone else who buys the category. Message tailoring by CEP is good;
audience shrinking is not.

### 7.4 Creative that works
- **Brand early and often:** people must know who the message is from, even if they only glance.
- **Use the distinctive assets.**
- **Link the brand to a CEP:** show the situation as well as the product.
- **Earn attention:** emotion, humour, surprise, story, characters, music. Not to "persuade", but to
  get noticed and remembered.
- **Keep it simple:** one idea, clearly branded.

### 7.5 Short and long term
Sales activation (offers, direct response) brings sales now. Brand building (reaching people who are
not buying yet) brings sales later. A small business needs both, but should never let activation
crowd out being remembered by future buyers.

## 8. Common myths, and what to do instead

| Myth | What the evidence shows | Do this instead |
|---|---|---|
| "Find your niche persona." | Your buyers look like the category's buyers. | Define the category and area; reach all of its buyers. |
| "Focus on loyal customers." | Growth comes from more buyers; loyalty follows size. | Keep customers happy, but invest in reaching new buyers. |
| "Be different (USP) or die." | Buyers rarely know how brands differ. Distinctiveness matters more. | Be distinctive and competitive; don't over-invest in being "unique". |
| "Discounts grow the business." | Promotions mainly reward existing buyers, briefly. | Use them sparingly; build availability instead. |
| "Run one big campaign." | Buyers enter the market all year. | Keep a steady, year-round presence. |
| "Change the logo to stay fresh." | Changing assets destroys recognition. | Keep assets; refresh the stories. |
| "Our customers love us, so they'll grow us." | Word of mouth follows size; love follows use. | Grow buyers; reviews and referrals follow. |
| "Measure success with likes." | Engagement among fans says little about new buyers. | Track new customers, reach and mental availability. |

## 9. What to measure

1. **Penetration:** how many different customers bought in a period, and how many are new. (The
   Studio reads this from your invoices.)
2. **Purchase frequency:** how often an average customer buys (expect it to rise only a little).
3. **Mental availability:** mental penetration, network size, mental market share, from a short
   survey once or twice a year.
4. **Distinctive asset strength:** fame and uniqueness per asset.
5. **Physical availability:** a checklist of presence, relevance and prominence.
6. **Reach and continuity:** how many category buyers your activities reached, and in how many
   months of the year you were present.
